How & Why 23 Timber Co. Started

Legacy · Stewardship · Origin

The number in the name isn't arbitrary. It comes from a specific moment in colonial history — and it still means something about who owns land, who decides what happens to it, and why that tension never really went away.

I've spent a lot of time in New England woodlots that were declining quietly — not from neglect exactly, but from a long series of decisions made without much connection to what the land actually needed. Selectively cut too many times. Left alone too long. Managed for short-term return without much thought about what came after. The results are predictable: poor regeneration, degraded habitat, stands full of the wrong species, access that hasn't been maintained in a generation.

What I kept running into, talking to the people who owned this land, was that they didn't want a logging job. They didn't want someone to come in, take what was valuable, and leave. They wanted to understand what they had and what to do with it over time — a plan that accounted for wildlife, timber, access, and what the property would look like in twenty or thirty years. That combination of things isn't something the industry serves particularly well. Loggers think about timber. Conservation organizations think about ecology. Hunters think about wildlife. The overlap between all three is where most private landowners actually live, and it's mostly unserved.

That's the gap 23 Timber Co. was built to fill. But the name itself comes from somewhere specific.

The white pine and the King's Broad Arrow

In the 17th and 18th centuries, the British Crown asserted ownership over all white pine trees in the American colonies measuring 24 inches in diameter or larger. These trees were marked with the King's Broad Arrow — three hatchet cuts in the shape of an arrowhead — and reserved exclusively for the Royal Navy. A mature white pine of that size made an exceptional ship's mast, and the Crown needed them badly enough to claim them on land it didn't otherwise control.

The problem was that these same trees were the backbone of colonial life. White pine built homes, heated them, provided lumber for trade, and generated income for the settlers who were working this land every day. The Crown's claim wasn't abstract — it meant that a farmer or woodsman could be fined, have timber confiscated, or face legal punishment for cutting a tree on his own property if it happened to meet the threshold.

Enforcement was inconsistent and the colonial courts were skeptical of the Crown's authority, but the resentment was real and it accumulated steadily over decades. What happened in response was practical rather than symbolic. Colonists began cutting trees to 23 inches. Just below the line. Not as a political statement — the statement came later — but because they needed the wood and they needed to stay on the right side of a rule they considered unjust. The inch was a workaround. A way of keeping what was theirs without direct confrontation.

What the timber conflict was actually about

The popular history of the American Revolution centers on tea and taxation, and those grievances were real. But the conflict over timber ran deeper and longer. When the Crown claimed those trees, it wasn't just regulating a natural resource — it was asserting authority over private land, local economies, and the basic right of people to use what they owned and worked for their own survival. The forests weren't background. They were the primary economic and physical resource of colonial New England, and controlling access to them was a form of control over everything else.

The resistance to that control didn't begin with the formal grievances of colonial assemblies. It began earlier and more quietly, in the woods, by people who were just trying to live on their land and make decisions about it without interference from a government three thousand miles away that had no relationship with the ground itself.

Why it still matters

The specific conflict is over. But the underlying tension — between people who live on land and institutions that make decisions about it from a distance — is still present in different forms. Regulations written without local knowledge. Conservation policies that treat private landowners as obstacles. Timber markets that reward extraction over stewardship. None of these are the same as the King's Broad Arrow, but they share the same basic structure: authority over land held by people who aren't accountable to the land or to the people managing it.

23 Timber Co. exists in the tradition of private landowners having real, informed agency over their property. Not as resistance for its own sake — the work operates entirely within current law and regulation — but as a commitment to the idea that good land decisions come from people who know the ground, not from people who've never walked it. The name is a reminder of where that idea came from and what it cost to establish it.

The settlers who cut those trees to 23 inches weren't making a grand gesture. They were solving a practical problem with the tools they had, on land they knew better than anyone, under circumstances they hadn't chosen. That's still the job.
