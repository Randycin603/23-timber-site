How & Why 23 Timber Co. Started

Legacy • Stewardship • Origin Story

Most companies in this industry start with equipment. 23 Timber Co. started with a question.

“What would the woods look like if people managed their land the way the land actually needs to be managed?”

For years, I watched New England woodlots decline — not from neglect, but from misunderstanding. Land that should’ve been producing wildlife, timber value, clean water, and generational memory instead became overgrown, unhealthy, or randomly cut without strategy.

At the same time, I kept having the same conversation with landowners:
They didn’t want a logging job. They didn’t want a landscaping job.
They wanted a plan.

A plan that respected:

  • Wildlife habitat
  • Timber value and rotation
  • Access, trails, and water
  • Fire behavior and risk reduction
  • The next generation — not just the next 12 months

But the industry is split: loggers focus on timber, conservation groups focus on ecology, hunters focus on wildlife… rarely do they overlap.

23 Timber Co. exists to sit directly in the middle of all three.

Where it really began

I grew up around land, machinery, and woods that told stories — but I didn’t fully appreciate how deep that connection ran until adulthood. Years of reading, working, burning, planting, learning, and observing slowly matured into a single mission:

“Build land plans that outlive their owners.”

Not plans that look good on paper. Plans that hold up in storms, droughts, winters, markets, wildlife pressure, time, and generations.

The number 23 in 23 Timber Co. isn’t branding trivia. It’s a reference to a quiet, practical act of resistance that shaped this country more than most people realize.

In the 17th and 18th centuries, the British Crown claimed ownership over all white pine trees measuring 24 inches in diameter and larger in the American colonies. These trees were marked with the King’s Broad Arrow and reserved exclusively for the Royal Navy — critical infrastructure for masts, spars, and shipbuilding.

To the Crown, these trees were assets.
To settlers, they were shelter, fuel, livelihood, and survival.

Colonial landowners were prohibited from cutting these trees on their own land. Violations carried heavy fines, confiscation, and punishment. Enforcement was inconsistent but real — and deeply resented.

So settlers adapted.

They cut trees to 23 inches.
Not out of symbolism, but necessity.

That single inch became a line in the sand. A workaround. A quiet refusal.

This Wasn’t About Tea

The popular narrative says the American Revolution was sparked by tea and taxes. Those mattered — but they weren’t the root.

The deeper conflict was about control of land, resources, and labor.

When the Crown claimed timber, it wasn’t just regulating forests. It was asserting authority over:

  • Private land
  • Local economies
  • Self-determination
  • The right to steward and use one’s own resources

Forests weren’t abstract. They were the backbone of colonial life. Restricting access to them meant restricting autonomy.

The resistance didn’t start with speeches or declarations.
It started with axes.

Why This Still Matters

That same tension still exists today — just dressed differently.

Centralized authority versus local stewardship.
Remote decision-makers versus people who live on the land.
Short-term extraction versus long-term care.

23 Timber Co. exists in that tradition.

Not as rebellion for its own sake — but as a commitment to:

  • Responsible land use
  • Long-term thinking
  • Practical stewardship
  • Private landowners having informed agency over their property

We work within the system.
But we don’t forget where that system came from — or who it was built for.

A Quiet Line, Still Drawn

The settlers who cut trees to 23 inches weren’t trying to start a revolution.

They were trying to live.

History remembers the explosions.
It forgets the inches.

We don’t.
